Where this came from

        
from sourceThe parser and the default envelope are ported from Isaiah Odhner's 1j01/rtttl.js — specifically rtttl.coffee and player.coffee, MIT licensed. Everything else here is new.

        
The parser keeps the original's two-tier approach: a strict pattern matching the Nokia spec, then a lenient fallback that accepts odd durations and octaves but raises a warning rather than failing. Spec defaults are d=4, o=6, b=63 when the control section leaves them out.

        
Note duration is (1/d) × 4 × (60/b) seconds, with each trailing dot multiplying by 1.5. Output was checked against an independent implementation: for Back to the Future, the first four events resolve to 450 ms at 784 Hz, a 75 ms rest, 450 ms at 523.3 Hz, another 75 ms rest — matching to the tenth of a hertz.

        
Scheduling

        
The whole tune is written to the Web Audio clock in one pass before playback starts, rather than being driven by timers. Timing is therefore sample-accurate and doesn't drift when the page is busy. The cost is that changing a control mid-song has to stop and reschedule.

      


      

        
Why a bare oscillator sounds harsh

        
Switching an oscillator on and off at full amplitude produces a step discontinuity in the waveform, and a step contains energy at every frequency. That's the click. It reads as harshness because it happens on every note.

        
from sourceThe sine voice uses the original fix: fade in over 1 ms, hold, fade out over the final 40 ms, then leave 10 ms of silence before the next note. The gap is what separates repeated notes at the same pitch — without it they slur into one long tone.

All four scale down proportionally on short notes, so a 32nd note at b=900 still gets a proportionate shape instead of an envelope longer than the note. The gate profile is deliberately close to a hard switch, because that's what a PWM pin does — the clicks it produces are the point, not an oversight.

        
Stop versus cancel

        
Because the whole tune is written to the audio clock in advance, halting it has two sensible meanings. Stop lets the note that's currently sounding play out to its own release, discards everything scheduled after it, and abandons a pending loop — so you hear a musical ending. Cancel discards the note in progress too, ramping to silence over 3 ms; inaudibly short, but enough to avoid the parting click that a true instant cut would produce.

      


      

        
The original is nine semitones sharp

        
from sourcertttl.js computes frequency as 440 × 2^(octave − 4 + index/12), where index is 0 for C. That makes C4 = 440 Hz. Under standard pitch C4 is 261.6 Hz and it's A4 that sits at 440.

        
Every note is shifted by the same interval, so relative pitch is intact and melodies are recognisable — they just play a major sixth above where they should. This bench defaults to 440 × 2^(octave − 4 + (index − 9)/12), which puts A4 at 440 Hz and matches what ESPHome sends to a buzzer.

        
The rtttl.js demo tuning option restores the original behaviour if you want to compare against that page directly.

        
Which octave RTTTL means

        
RTTTL octave numbers line up with scientific pitch notation, so o=5, note a is 880 Hz. Most tunes in the wild are written for o=5 or o=6, which matters a lot once a highpass filter is in the chain — see the Voices tab.

      


      

        
What a voice is

        
One or more oscillators at fixed ratios of the note frequency, a gain envelope, and a fixed filter chain standing in for the physical transducer. The filter chain is where the character lives.

Why a highpass matters so much

        
A small transducer can't move air at low frequencies, so its fundamental below roughly a kilohertz simply doesn't come out. What you hear instead is the note's harmonics. That means a tune written in o=5 can sound an octave up, thin and detached, on hardware while sounding fine in a browser — and it's the single most common reason a transcription that plays nicely on a laptop disappoints on a device.

        
Pulse waves

        
The pulse voices are built from Fourier coefficients (2/(nπ) · sin(nπd) for 96 harmonics) rather than a naive square, so the browser band-limits them and they don't alias into garbage at high octaves. Duty cycle changes the harmonic balance: 50% cancels all even harmonics, narrower duties get progressively more nasal and quieter.

      


      

        
What's confirmed

        
from docsApollo Automation devices with a buzzer — BTN-1, MSR-2, MTR-1, TEMP-1, PLT-1, R PRO-1, AIR-1 and the holiday ornaments — all use a passive piezo element, driven by ESPHome's rtttl component on an ESP32 ledc PWM output. Their reference configuration is a plain output: platform: ledc feeding rtttl:. So the signal reaching the element is a hard-gated pulse train, not a shaped tone.

        
What's estimated

        
estimatedApollo don't publish the element, its resonant frequency, or the enclosure acoustics anywhere I could find, and their wiki doesn't name a part. The filter chain models the class of component they're visibly using — a small element behind a narrow port in a tight plastic shell:

        
    
          
  • Highpass, 1.8 kHz — a small piezo produces essentially nothing below this, so notes in o=4 and o=5 arrive as harmonics only.
    • 
          
  • +7 dB at 2.7 kHz, Q 3 — the enclosure cavity mode.
    • 
          
  • +20 dB at 4.2 kHz, Q 6 — the element's own resonance. The high Q is why each note-off rings briefly.
    • 
          
  • Lowpass, 11 kHz — the top end rolls away.
    • 
        

        
The audible signature is that notes are wildly uneven in loudness: anything near 4 kHz stabs, anything much below o=6 sounds thin and far away. That unevenness is the real-world behaviour worth designing around.

        
PWM duty and ESPHome's gain

        
The duty slider is the analogue of gain: in an ESPHome rtttl block. On a piezo it isn't really a volume control — it's a duty-cycle control, so lowering it thins the tone and shifts harmonic balance as much as it reduces level. 60% is the default here because that's what most community configurations use.

        
If you have one of these on the bench, play a sustained note, find where the peak actually sits, and the 4.2 kHz figure is a one-line change in the apollo voice definition.

      


      

        
Known gaps

        
    
          
  • LEDC frequency quantisation. The ESP32 can only produce frequencies its clock divider and bit resolution allow, so real hardware lands slightly off-pitch, increasingly so at high notes. This bench plays exact frequencies.
    • 
          
  • Enclosure resonance. Whatever you mount a disc into moves the resonant peak, sometimes by a lot. A bare disc on a bench and the same disc in a case are different instruments.
    • 
          
  • Filter ringing is not physical ringing. A high-Q biquad rings after the gate closes, which sounds convincingly like a piezo, but it's a coincidence of similar mathematics rather than a mechanical model.
    • 
          
  • No drive nonlinearity. A piezo driven hard from a GPIO pin distorts and can be current-limited. Nothing here clips or saturates.
    • 
          
  • ESPHome note timing. Its inter-note behaviour isn't reproduced exactly; the gate profile is a close approximation, not a port.
    • 
          
  • Your playback chain. Laptop speakers roll off low frequencies too, which can mask exactly the effect these voices are demonstrating. Headphones give a truer comparison.
    • 
          
  • Monophonic only. RTTTL is a single-voice format and so is this.
    • 
        

        
Parser edge cases

        
Non-ASCII characters in a data section will fail the note match and stop parsing with an error naming the token — a few tunes in circulation contain them. Off-spec durations and octaves are accepted with a warning rather than rejected, so the message line below the text box is worth reading when something sounds wrong.
